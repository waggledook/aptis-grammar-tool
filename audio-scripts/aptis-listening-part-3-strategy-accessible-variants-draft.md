# Aptis Listening Part 3 more accessible conversation variants

Draft for review, 23 November 2026.

Six alternative scripts provide a more accessible route through selected conversations in the [main draft](aptis-listening-part-3-strategy-lesson-draft.md). They retain its student statements and correct answers, so a teacher can choose either version of a conversation. The opening name-badge model in the main draft can serve both routes.

These variants make the evidence more local, reduce the number of competing clues and express some positions more openly. Students still need to understand paraphrase, distinguish the exact claim from a related point and listen to both speakers. Normal conversational delivery is appropriate for both banks.

## Choosing a route

Use the following as teacher options when selecting material. The labels describe support within the scripts, rather than fixed student levels.

| Route | Suggested selection | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More support | Shared model, then these versions of Extracts 2, 5, 4, 7, 6 and 9 | Establish the distinctions with clearer positions before combining them in two statements |
| More inference | Main-bank extracts, with the shared model if useful | Infer positions from less explicit evidence and track competing points across turns |
| Mixed selection | Choose one version per conversation according to the skill the class needs | Provide more support for a particular distinction without lowering every item |

For a fresh check of readiness for the harder material, use a different conversation that students have not heard, such as main-bank Extract 3, 8 or 10. The paired versions deliberately share statements and answers: hearing the more accessible version may reveal the answer to its harder counterpart. If both are used, treat the second as a comparison of how the opinion is expressed, rather than as an independent assessment.

## Teacher scripts and feedback

Read or record only the dialogue. Use the same two distinguishable voices and a similar natural pace as the main bank. Easier attribution should come from the script, without exaggerated stress on the answer or additional pauses around decisive phrases. Most single-statement versions are roughly 20–30 seconds; the film discussion is roughly 45–60 seconds, subject to recorded delivery.

### Extract 2 A pottery workshop

**Statement:** The pottery workshop costs more than it is worth.

**Dialogue**

**Man:** I liked making the bowl, but for thirty-five pounds I expected more time using the wheel. We spent nearly half the afternoon waiting.

**Woman:** There was some waiting. For me, learning to shape the clay was the point. I'd pay that price again, even if I didn't take a bowl home.

**Answer:** Man.

**Evidence and explanation:** The man regards the practical time as insufficient for the price, despite enjoying the activity. The woman acknowledges the waiting, but values learning enough to pay the same price again. She is not agreeing with his judgement of value.

**What remains to interpret:** Enjoyment does not necessarily mean good value; acknowledging a drawback does not necessarily mean poor value.

**Why this is more accessible:** The cost and practical time are connected in one turn. The woman gives her reason for paying again directly. There is no competing comparison between the sale values of two bowls, and no later condition to integrate.

**Editorial attribution rating:** 2, compared with 3 in the main bank.

**After listening:** Which point does the woman accept, and which judgement does she not share?

### Extract 4 Booking a haircut

**Statement:** Customers who have always kept their appointments should not have to pay the booking deposit.

**Dialogue**

**Woman:** Rosa can ask for a deposit from people who've missed appointments. But I've kept every booking for six years. I should be able to put my name in the diary without paying first.

**Man:** My sister says the same. I'd keep one rule for everyone, though. Otherwise Rosa has to decide which customers she can trust, and explain why one pays and another doesn't.

**Answer:** Woman.

**Evidence and explanation:** The woman accepts deposits for customers who have missed appointments, but argues that her own record should allow her to book without paying. The man's sister shares that view. His own preference is a uniform rule, which does not exempt reliable customers.

**What remains to interpret:** The man reports an opinion before expressing a different one. The woman's acceptance of deposits for some people does not include the group in the statement.

**Why this is more accessible:** The woman's preferred exception is stated directly, and the man's personal view immediately follows the reported view. The separate argument about lost income has been removed.

**Editorial attribution rating:** 2, compared with 3–4 in the main bank.

**After listening:** Who agrees with the woman in the man's first sentence? What does he want himself?

### Extract 5 Meal kits

**Statement:** Meal kits reduce the time needed to prepare an evening meal.

**Dialogue**

**Man:** The ingredients came in separate portions. I had everything in the pan while I'd normally still be weighing things.

**Woman:** I used mine on late work nights for that reason. The actual cooking wasn't quicker, but there was less to do beforehand. I stopped ordering because of the price.

**Answer:** Both.

**Evidence and explanation:** The man contrasts getting ingredients into the pan with still measuring them in his usual routine. The woman also values the reduced preparation, even though cooking itself takes the same time. Her reason for cancelling is cost.

**What remains to interpret:** Distinguish getting ingredients ready from cooking them, and a reservation about price from the judgement about preparation time.

**Why this is more accessible:** Each speaker's time comparison is local. Students do not have to reconstruct the forty-minute and twenty-minute comparison or retain it through a later exchange about washing-up.

**Editorial attribution rating:** 2, compared with 3 in the main bank.

**After listening:** What does the woman say was not quicker? Does that contradict the statement?

### Extract 6 A new escape room

**Statement:** The new escape room is a good choice for a group of complete beginners.

**Dialogue**

**Man:** I'm booking the new escape room for our team. They've never done one before, but the puzzles don't need any special knowledge.

**Woman:** That part is fine. But beginners won't know when to ask for clues. I'd only book it for people who already know how escape rooms work.

**Answer:** Man.

**Evidence and explanation:** The man chooses the activity for first-time participants because of the accessible puzzles. The woman accepts that assessment of the puzzles, but limits her recommendation to people familiar with the activity. She does not extend it to complete beginners.

**What remains to interpret:** The woman's initial positive response concerns puzzle knowledge, not the suitability of the whole activity for the team.

**Why this is more accessible:** Her restriction is explicit and appears in one turn. The dialogue does not reconstruct the earlier hint-button experience or require students to resolve why offering an explanation on request fails to answer her concern.

**Editorial attribution rating:** 2–3, compared with 3–4 in the main bank.

**After listening:** What does that part refer to? Which people would the woman book it for?

### Extract 7 A personalised present

**Statement:** A personalised present is worth waiting longer for.

**Dialogue**

**Woman:** An ordinary mug would arrive tomorrow. The one with Dad's old boat on it takes three weeks, but I'd rather wait for something that means more to him.

**Man:** He'll like that picture. Do they let you check the drawing before they print it?

**Woman:** Yes. They send it to me first.

**Answer:** Woman.

**Evidence and explanation:** The woman explicitly prefers a longer wait for a more meaningful gift. The man predicts a positive reaction to the picture and asks about approving it. Neither remark establishes whether he considers the extra wait worthwhile.

**What remains to interpret:** A positive response to the design is not automatically an opinion about the delivery trade-off. The man's position on the exact statement remains unclear, rather than opposed.

**Why this is more accessible:** The woman gives the comparison and preference in one turn. The seller's claim, missed-birthday implication and colleague's interest have been removed. The man still contributes a relevant positive response, so students must identify its scope.

**Editorial attribution rating:** 2–3, compared with 3 in the main bank.

**After listening:** Which part of the present does the man comment on? Does he evaluate the extra wait?

### Extract 9 An outdoor film screening

**Statements**

- **9a:** Seats should be reserved when tickets are booked.
- **9b:** Sellers should bring snacks to people in their seats while the film is playing.

**Dialogue**

**Woman:** I'd let people reserve a seat when they buy a ticket for the outdoor film.

**Man:** I was going to let them choose when they arrive.

**Woman:** Last year people saved chairs for friends, and the volunteers had to sort out arguments.

**Man:** Then I'd rather they chose when they book. It would avoid that problem. What about taking snacks round during the film, so nobody misses a scene?

**Woman:** Selling them before the film is fine. During it, the seller would stand in front of the screen.

**Man:** People could pay in advance, so the seller wouldn't have to count change.

**Woman:** That would be quicker, but someone would still be blocking the view.

**Answers:** 9a Both; 9b Man.

**9a evidence and explanation:** The woman proposes booking seats in advance. The man first favours choosing on arrival, then explicitly changes his preference after hearing about the arguments. His reason shows that he supports the policy, rather than merely agreeing to carry it out.

**9b evidence and explanation:** The man proposes bringing snacks to seats during the film. The woman accepts sales before the film, but objects to blocking the screen during it. Her later acceptance that advance payment would be quicker does not remove that objection.

**What remains to interpret:** Use the man's revised opinion for the seating question. For the snack question, distinguish before from during, and a concession about speed from acceptance of the service.

**Why this is more accessible:** The seating discussion is completed before the snack discussion starts. The man's change of preference is explicit. The woman states her objection directly instead of initially expressing it as a challenging question.

**Editorial attribution ratings:** 9a 2–3, compared with 4 in the main bank; 9b 2–3, compared with 3–4.

**After listening:** Why is the man's first seating answer no longer his answer? What is the woman accepting in her last turn?

## Teacher comparison

The same editorial scale is used in both drafts: 1 direct, 2 straightforward, 3 demanding and 4 challenging. These ratings estimate attribution from the text; they do not establish CEFR levels or measured difficulty.

| Item | Answer | More accessible version | Main version | Support provided by the alternative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Man | 2 | 3 | Direct connection between cost, practical time and willingness to pay again |
| 4 | Woman | 2 | 3–4 | Reported and personal views appear together; one argument rather than several |
| 5 | Both | 2 | 3 | Local preparation-time comparison, with one separate reservation |
| 6 | Man | 2–3 | 3–4 | Explicit limit on who the woman recommends it for |
| 7 | Woman | 2–3 | 3 | Explicit preference for waiting, without additional sources or timing implications |
| 9a | Both | 2–3 | 4 | Immediate resolution of the first topic and explicit change of preference |
| 9b | Man | 2–3 | 3–4 | Direct objection and one clearly marked concession |

## Recording and review

Keep the pace and voice quality comparable across versions so that teachers can judge the difference in attribution demands. Retain natural contractions and short conversational turns. Explain unfamiliar setting vocabulary before either version if needed.

Use first-listen answers, second-listen answers and students' reasons to review the material after recording. Check that students still understand the key distinction, rather than succeeding only by hearing a familiar agreement word. The more accessible route should prepare them to interpret less explicit evidence in a different, unseen conversation.
