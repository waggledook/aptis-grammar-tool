# OTE Advanced Listening Part 2: four drafts for review

Prepared: 5 November 2026

Original practice material. Draft C1 open note-completion tasks; not official Oxford material or calibrated exam items. Text only, ready for editorial review.

Each task contains a lecture, student notes, and a teacher key with the relevant evidence. The examples are illustrative teaching scenarios, not reports of named experiments or actual park projects. Sources for background checking are listed at the end, outside the student material.

Suggested exam-style delivery: 30 seconds to preview the notes, two plays of the lecture, then 15 seconds to check answers. Read the task introduction separately from the lecture. Word counts below apply to the lecture only.

## 1. What makes practice effective in sport

### Student task

Listen to a lecture about effective practice in sport. Complete the gaps in the notes with a word or two-word phrase from the audio. Remember to check your spelling.

The clock shows how much time you have to look at the task.

**Effective practice in sport**

**Defining success**

1. Effective practice develops the __________ needed during competition.

**Planning activities**

2. Coaches can assess accuracy by introducing smaller __________.
3. Reviewing __________ can reveal changes missed during a session.
4. Changing players' __________ introduces variety without replacing the skill.

**Increasing independence**

5. A player's own assessment can come before the coach's __________.

**Checking learning**

6. Skills should eventually be tested under __________.

### Lecture

Imagine watching two tennis players practise. One receives the same comfortable ball again and again, returning almost every shot successfully. The other has to move, judge an unfamiliar bounce and occasionally miss. From the side of the court, the first session might look more impressive. But a useful question is what each player will be able to do when the predictable supply of balls disappears. Today, I'd like to explore the difference between producing a good performance in practice and preparing to perform elsewhere.

Repetition has a place, particularly when someone is becoming familiar with a movement. Competition, however, rarely presents an action in isolation. A player must notice where an opponent is standing and select an appropriate response. Practice therefore needs to develop decisions as well as movements. Someone who can perform a shot beautifully when told exactly what to do may still struggle to recognise the moment to use it.

That distinction affects how an activity is planned. Consider a coach working on accurate passing. Simply asking for more effort gives players little to work towards. Placing target areas on the court creates a visible measure of where the ball arrives. These can gradually be made smaller. Counting successful passes is useful, although that number alone cannot show whether the movement is becoming easier or more controlled.

There are other ways to examine a change. A player's memory of a session may be dominated by its final few minutes. Video allows an earlier attempt to be compared with a later one, including changes that nobody noticed at the time. A written score can show how often something worked; a moving image can help show how it worked. Neither needs to become a judgement on the player's overall ability.

Variation also requires care. Replacing an exercise every few minutes may be entertaining without giving anyone time to understand it. Instead, a coach can retain the basic task but alter the starting positions. Players still work on passing, yet they must adjust to receiving the ball from somewhere different. Introducing an opponent later adds another decision. The intention is to change a meaningful demand rather than merely create novelty.

Another consideration is when the coach speaks. Before offering feedback, the coach might ask a player where the last attempt went and what they would change. This gives the player an opportunity to interpret the result independently. If instructions arrive after every attempt, the next action may depend on waiting for another instruction. A conversation about one specific feature can be more manageable than a list of everything that went wrong.

Finally, a skill that works in a quiet exercise needs a further test under pressure. Keeping score or limiting the available time can introduce consequences without reproducing an entire match. Mistakes then become information about what still needs work. The most useful practice session is not necessarily the one that looks most successful while it is happening.

### Teacher key and review notes

1. **decisions** — The lecture adds recognising and selecting responses to executing movements. Competing detail: movements. The note requires what practice develops for competitive situations.
2. **target areas** — These make accuracy visible and can be reduced in size. Competing detail: the number of successful passes, which is a measure rather than a physical target. Do not accept "areas" alone: it loses the defining function.
3. **video** — Also accept **moving image**, which is used verbatim later in the same paragraph. Both distinguish the answer from a written score and personal memory. The note condenses comparing early and late attempts.
4. **starting positions** — This changes the conditions while preserving the underlying passing task. Adding an opponent is a subsequent variation. "Positions" is an acceptable shorter response because the note supplies the players and the action of changing.
5. **feedback** — The answer occurs before the explanation that establishes the player's assessment comes first. "Instructions" describes a contrasting pattern, not the specific exchange in the note.
6. **pressure** — Keeping score and limiting time exemplify the broader condition required. Neither individual example completes the note naturally.

Design emphasis: distinguish immediate performance from learning; match measures to their purposes; resolve a relationship explained after the answer. The six answers mix single nouns with two-word phrases. No universal claim is made that one practice method is best for every learner or every stage.

## 2. How theme parks turn waiting into part of the attraction

### Student task

Listen to a lecture about theme park queue design. Complete the gaps in the notes with a word or two-word phrase from the audio. Remember to check your spelling.

The clock shows how much time you have to look at the task.

**Designing a theme park queue**

**Revealing the setting**

1. Designers can use __________ to reveal scenes in stages.
2. A workshop's __________ can communicate information without a formal explanation.

**Making the route comfortable**

3. An outdoor route may need __________ more urgently than additional entertainment.

**Maintaining interest**

4. Designers must consider the effect of __________ during longer waits.

**Preparing for the ride**

5. Information near the __________ can help groups board efficiently.
6. The queue helps shape visitors' __________ about the experience ahead.

### Lecture

When does a theme park attraction begin? You might say it starts when the vehicle moves. A designer may give a different answer: the experience can begin as soon as visitors leave the main path and join the queue. Waiting remains waiting, of course. A decorated corridor does not increase the number of seats on a ride. Nevertheless, the route can introduce a setting, prepare people for what follows and give them something worth noticing along the way.

One useful design question concerns how much visitors can see at once. A straight passage presents its contents immediately, whereas corners allow a setting to appear in stages. Imagine approaching a fictional explorer's workshop. A glimpse of a damaged machine could be followed by a room showing the tools used to repair it. Each new view develops the situation. This is different from simply concealing how far the queue extends: the sequence needs to remain interesting once someone reaches the next section.

The workshop need not explain its history through a long announcement. Background details can suggest who works there and what has recently happened. A half-packed case might imply a hurried departure; a collection of unfinished inventions could suggest repeated failure. Visitors can interpret these clues while moving. Spoken dialogue may add another layer, but it should not carry every essential point, since someone arriving halfway through a recording will have missed its beginning.

Physical comfort still matters. In a hypothetical outdoor section, designers might be deciding between another animated figure and a covered walkway. Shade could be the more valuable addition on a hot afternoon. Visitors who are concentrating on finding relief from the sun may pay little attention to an elaborate scene. Seating where appropriate, space for groups and a clear route also influence whether people can enjoy what has been created around them.

An effect that works beautifully on a brief walkthrough may become irritating during a slow queue. A talking portrait could deliver its joke just as a group passes, or the same group could hear it six times. Repetition is therefore something designers need to test, alongside volume and visibility. A short story with a fixed sequence also creates difficulties if visitors move out of the room before its ending. Some details can instead be discovered independently, in any order.

The approach to the loading area has a different purpose. Here, visitors need to understand where to stand, how their group will be seated and when to move. Instructions delivered at this point can prevent hesitation when a vehicle arrives. Complicated storytelling should leave room for this practical information. A beautifully staged final scene is of limited value if it prevents people hearing the boarding directions.

Ultimately, the queue shapes expectations. A playful introduction prepares visitors differently from a tense sequence suggesting that something has gone wrong. Neither approach is automatically better; what matters is its relationship to the ride that follows. The design succeeds when the experience feels connected, rather than when the waiting space merely contains the greatest number of effects.

### Teacher key and review notes

1. **corners** — They enable successive views. A straight passage is the comparison. The note paraphrases the idea of a setting appearing in stages.
2. **background details** — Objects communicate aspects of the workshop's history. "Details" is also acceptable in this note. An announcement and dialogue are other means of communication, but neither completes this exact claim.
3. **shade** — The illustrative choice concerns outdoor comfort. An animated figure provides entertainment, not the relief required by the note.
4. **repetition** — The talking portrait illustrates the problem. Volume and visibility are additional testing considerations; the qualification about longer waits selects the repeated effect.
5. **loading area** — This is the location linked to standing, seating and movement. "Area" alone is too imprecise. The answer precedes the explanation of why instructions there support boarding.
6. **expectations** — Playful and tense introductions establish different anticipations. The answer names what the queue shapes; it does not ask for either mood.

Design emphasis: staged revelation, environmental storytelling and the fit between queue and ride. The focus deliberately differs from the museum task's arrival patterns, queue fairness and disruption management. All park situations in the lecture are illustrative rather than attributed to a real attraction.

## 3. Why people keep or abandon a new hobby

### Student task

Listen to a lecture about continuing with a new hobby. Complete the gaps in the notes with a word or two-word phrase from the audio. Remember to check your spelling.

The clock shows how much time you have to look at the task.

**Continuing with a hobby**

**Expectations and access**

1. Beginners may judge themselves against other people's __________.
2. Reducing __________ makes brief sessions easier to fit into daily life.

**Recognising development**

3. Listening to earlier __________ helps musicians assess changes over time.

**Personal involvement**

4. Having __________ can make an activity feel more personally meaningful.
5. For some participants, __________ matters more than further improvement.

**Deciding whether to continue**

6. Stopping may reflect a change in __________ rather than a lack of determination.

### Lecture

A cupboard containing unused paints or a musical instrument gathering dust can look like evidence of failed determination. Yet the decision to continue with a hobby involves more than being a disciplined person. Someone may enjoy an activity but struggle to fit it into daily life; another person may practise regularly and discover that the experience offers little pleasure. Today, I'd like to consider what can happen between the excitement of beginning and the decision about whether to carry on.

One difficulty is the comparison available to a beginner. An online search for pottery may produce hundreds of beautiful finished objects, with little indication of the unsuccessful attempts behind them. These can become the standard against which someone's first bowl is judged. The problem is not necessarily that the beginner expected no difficulties. It may be that they have seen almost nothing representing the ordinary stages between starting and becoming skilled.

Practical arrangements also deserve attention. Imagine someone who has twenty minutes available for drawing but must first clear a table, locate materials and arrange a lamp. Preparation takes up much of the opportunity. Leaving a small drawing kit somewhere accessible changes what can happen in that same period. Buying a more expensive set of pencils would not solve the particular obstacle. Repeating an activity in a familiar situation can also make beginning it less dependent on a fresh decision each time.

Progress presents another complication: becoming more aware of errors can feel like getting worse. A person learning an instrument might begin to hear unevenness that previously passed unnoticed. Recordings offer a way of comparing performances made several weeks apart, rather than relying entirely on today's impression. The point is not to publish them or produce a professional result. They preserve evidence of an earlier stage that memory may have made unrealistically flattering.

There is also a difference between following a programme and feeling that it belongs to you. Choice matters here. A teacher might provide a small selection of pieces that practise the same musical skill, allowing a learner to select one they actually want to play. The technical demand remains. What changes is the learner's relationship to it. Unlimited freedom is not required, and abandoning all structure may leave a beginner uncertain about what to do next.

Improvement is not everyone's main reason for returning. Company may be the attraction of a weekly walking group, even if nobody is interested in increasing the distance or pace. For someone who values time alone, the same arrangement could make the activity less appealing. We should therefore be cautious about treating competition, public performance or group membership as an automatic source of motivation.

Finally, priorities change. A hobby that once offered a welcome challenge may no longer suit someone's circumstances or interests. Money already spent on equipment cannot establish whether continuing is worthwhile now. There is a difference between leaving because one disappointing session felt conclusive and leaving after discovering that another activity is more rewarding. Persistence can be valuable, but understanding why we return is more useful than assuming that every departure represents failure.

### Teacher key and review notes

1. **finished objects** — These supply an unrepresentative comparison because the unsuccessful attempts and intermediate stages are missing. "Objects" alone does not identify their completed state.
2. **preparation** — The drawing example links time spent setting up with difficulty using a short opportunity. Pencil quality is irrelevant to that obstacle.
3. **recordings** — They preserve earlier performances for comparison. Today's impression and memory are less reliable alternatives in the example. Publishing is explicitly not the purpose.
4. **choice** — The music example supplies bounded options while maintaining the technical requirement. Neither complete freedom nor removal of structure is the principle described.
5. **company** — The walking example contrasts social enjoyment with increasing distance or pace. The following qualification acknowledges people who prefer solitude.
6. **priorities** — Changing circumstances and the appeal of another activity explain the conclusion. The cost of equipment is a competing consideration, not the reason the note identifies.

Design emphasis: distinguish practical barriers from lack of interest; recognise progress versus the perception of progress; connect a concrete example to an abstract noun. The final section avoids treating continued participation as the only successful outcome.

## 4. How sound effects are created for films

### Student task

Listen to a lecture about creating film sound effects. Complete the gaps in the notes with a word or two-word phrase from the audio. Remember to check your spelling.

The clock shows how much time you have to look at the task.

**Creating sounds for a film**

**Recording priorities**

1. Recording on set often gives priority to __________.

**Choosing materials**

2. __________ can be moved to recreate the sound of a costume.
3. The studio may provide __________ for a scene filmed on a path.

**Performing and combining sounds**

4. A convincing effect depends partly on accurate __________.
5. Keeping sounds on __________ makes later adjustments easier.

**Directing attention**

6. Deliberate use of __________ can make a small sound more noticeable.

### Lecture

Think of a scene in which a character enters an empty house. You hear a key turn, a coat brush against a doorway and a cautious footstep. It is tempting to imagine that a microphone simply captured all of this during filming. In practice, a soundtrack can combine recordings made in different places and at different stages. Today, I'll focus on the small, performed effects that help give an action its physical presence on screen.

During filming, dialogue often receives priority. An actor's words need to be clear, while surrounding sounds may be inconvenient, inconsistent or impossible to control. A footstep that seems distinct to someone standing beside the actor might be barely audible on the recording. Later, performers can recreate selected actions while watching the image. This work is known as Foley, after an early practitioner of the technique. It supplements production sound rather than necessarily replacing everything recorded on location.

The material used need not be the original object. Fabric can be rubbed, stretched or folded to suggest the movement of a costume. The artist listens for the weight and texture that fit the character's clothes, perhaps rejecting a piece that sounds too stiff. Matching the colour or appearance of the original garment would contribute nothing to the recording. What matters is the result when it accompanies the image.

Footsteps require another set of choices. A studio can provide gravel for a path seen in the picture, along with other surfaces for different scenes. Selecting suitable shoes is only part of the job. An anxious character may place each foot carefully; someone in a hurry might drag a heel or land heavily. The performer is recreating behaviour as well as contact between a shoe and the ground. The same equipment can consequently produce very different impressions.

Timing connects that performance to the picture. A convincing footstep arriving slightly before the foot touches down can expose the construction of the scene. The artist watches the action, repeats the movement and adjusts it until the two belong together. Changing the recording level cannot repair every mismatch. A sound may have exactly the right texture and still fail because it occurs at the wrong moment.

Sounds are often recorded on separate tracks, allowing an editor to adjust one without altering everything else. Imagine a character dropping a bag and then opening it. The impact, a buckle and the movement of its contents might each need different treatment. Combining them too early would make a later request difficult: lowering the buckle could also weaken the impact. Recording several versions gives the editor alternatives, although collecting more material is not a substitute for choosing carefully.

Finally, a believable soundtrack does not require every visible movement to be equally audible. Silence can draw attention to a tiny action that would otherwise disappear among competing sounds. In the empty house, a single key turning may carry more tension than a continuous collection of creaks. The craft involves deciding what the audience should notice, as well as discovering how to make it.

### Teacher key and review notes

1. **dialogue** — The lecture explains the filming priority before discussing effects recreated later. Footsteps are an example of something that may not be captured clearly.
2. **fabric** — The answer is followed by actions demonstrating how it recreates costume movement. Colour and appearance are rejected selection criteria, not materials to reproduce.
3. **gravel** — This is the studio surface corresponding to the outdoor setting. Shoes are separately identified as another part of the performance.
4. **timing** — The foot touching down supplies the visual event that the sound must match. Texture and recording level cannot by themselves resolve the mismatch.
5. **separate tracks** — The bag example explains the advantage after naming the arrangement. "Tracks" alone is acceptable because the note already supplies "Keeping sounds on" and the distinction is clear in context.
6. **silence** — The final example links reduced competition with increased attention to the key. Creaks are the competing sounds, not the organising choice the note asks for.

Design emphasis: distinguish recording stages; separate the material from the criterion for choosing it; understand a technical workflow through ordinary language. "Foley" is explained but is not a tested answer. The house, costume and bag sequences are invented illustrations.
