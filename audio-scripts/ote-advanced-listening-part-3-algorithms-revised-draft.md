# OTE Advanced Listening Part 3 — Algorithms and cultural discovery

Draft 2 — 11 November 2026 — for editorial review before recording.

Original practice material. The programme, speakers and personal examples are fictional.

Dialogue: 621 words, excluding speaker labels and task instructions. At 150–160 words per minute, allow approximately four minutes per play, plus natural pauses.

## Student task

Listen to a music journalist and a media researcher discussing personalised music recommendations. Match the people—the woman, the man, or both—to the opinions below.

The clock shows how much time you have to look at the task.

Preparation: 30 seconds. Play the dialogue twice, then allow 15 seconds to check answers. Record the task instructions separately from the dialogue.

### Opinions

1. A list of unfamiliar performers can exaggerate the musical variety being offered.
2. Unfamiliar music is better introduced when listeners can give it their full attention.
3. Listening records can give a misleading picture of someone's musical preferences.
4. Guidance from another person can make an initially unappealing piece worth revisiting.
5. Listeners should be able to choose how far recommendations depart from their existing tastes.
6. A closer match to the listener's tastes should always take precedence over promoting a new artist.

## Dialogue

**Woman:** Welcome to Culture in the Feed. I'm music journalist Nora Ellis, with media researcher Dr Samir Malik. Samir, I've been looking through my recommended playlists. Hardly a name I recognise in some of them. Whatever else we say about these services, they do seem to get us beyond the usual suspects.

**Man:** Beyond the names, certainly. The last one I checked had about twenty new performers, all doing rather similar things. Change the voices and the sleeve pictures and you've still got much the same musical territory. An impressive-looking list of discoveries, on paper.

**Woman:** A different voice can transform a song, though. I wouldn't need a playlist to cross three continents before calling it varied. Some of my most interesting finds have been people doing something distinctive within a style I already know.

**Man:** And would you put one of those lists on while working?

**Woman:** That's when I reach for something familiar. If I'm answering emails, a new singer could do something extraordinary and it would pass me by. I'd save the introduction for an evening when I could actually hear what made them worth recommending.

**Man:** My working playlist has turned up a few surprises. One track kept catching my attention between emails; eventually I looked up the singer. I doubt I'd have chosen to sit down with it beforehand. Sometimes letting something in at the edges is how it gets a proper hearing later.

**Woman:** Although the service can't know which part of that process a play represents. My account still has an enthusiasm for a singer my niece used for dance rehearsals. She played one song repeatedly. I've never chosen it myself.

**Man:** There's a similar problem with the album I leave running at night. It must look like a favourite. Ask me about the music I care about and it wouldn't make the list. A completed play is a convenient thing to count.

**Woman:** A friend sent me an album recently with a note about the way the bass kept interrupting the melody. The first track had done nothing for me. I went back to it with that in mind, and ended up listening to the whole thing. The playlist had offered me the same album weeks before.

**Man:** I remember a critic describing a quartet I'd abandoned as a question that only gets answered in the final bars. I tried again, listening for that connection. By the end, the opening made rather more sense. Still not something I'd put on every day.

**Woman:** Could services offer that sort of introduction?

**Man:** They could try. I'd also like some say in how far they take me from familiar ground. A choice between staying nearby and going further would do; I don't need twenty controls.

**Woman:** Yes, those endless settings pages used to be dreadful. The service I use has cleared most of that away now. But even with two buttons, I'd be deciding what sort of discovery I wanted before I'd made it. I'd rather be offered something and react to it.

**Man:** You could leave the choice untouched. I'd use it, particularly when a run of safe suggestions had started to feel like being stuck in the same room.

**Woman:** And somewhere outside that room there's a new artist hoping to get in. I spend half my working life introducing people to them. On a personal service, though, if an established artist is a closer fit, that's who belongs in the list. Someone else's need for an audience shouldn't reverse that choice.

**Man:** The established artist will win most comparisons like that. I'd reserve a few places for newcomers even when the match is weaker. Otherwise their lack of listeners becomes the reason they never get any.

**Woman:** That's where we'll have to leave it. Samir, thank you.

**Man:** Thank you.

## Teacher key and attribution notes

**Key: 1 Man · 2 Woman · 3 Both · 4 Both · 5 Man · 6 Woman.**

### 1. Man

The man contrasts twenty unfamiliar performers with substantially unchanged musical territory. The woman regards a distinctive voice within a familiar style as meaningful variety. Her opening observation about unfamiliar names introduces his distinction; it does not endorse his judgement that the list exaggerates variety.

**Attractive wrong answer: Both.** Both discuss unfamiliar performers and discovery, but they evaluate what counts as musical variety differently.

### 2. Woman

The woman would postpone introducing a new singer until she could attend to what made the recommendation worthwhile. The man describes an introduction that succeeded during distracted listening and led to closer attention later. He values that route because he would probably not have selected the track for a dedicated listening session.

**Attractive wrong answer: Both.** His later close listening is an outcome of the introduction, not a condition he says should precede it. The statement concerns when unfamiliar music is first introduced, rather than whether it eventually rewards attention.

### 3. Both

The woman's repeated plays were generated by her niece. The man's completed plays come from an album he leaves running at night but does not regard as a favourite. Each independently supplies evidence that a listening record can misrepresent personal preferences.

**Attractive wrong answer: Woman.** The man's use of the album is deliberate, but that does not make its play count a reliable measure of his musical attachment. Neither speaker claims listening data is always useless.

### 4. Both

The woman's friend's note prompts a second attempt at a track she initially found unrewarding. The critic's description gives the man a reason to return to a quartet he had abandoned. The explanations change their engagement with the music, without either speaker announcing agreement with the other.

**Attractive wrong answer: Woman.** The man's qualification that the quartet would not become everyday listening limits how much he likes it; it does not cancel the value of revisiting it. The statement does not require a recommendation to create a favourite.

### 5. Man

The man proposes an optional choice about distance from familiar music, then explains when he would use it. The woman agrees that old settings pages were poor and acknowledges that her current service has simplified them. She still prefers reacting to actual suggestions rather than specifying the desired degree of discovery in advance.

**Attractive wrong answer: Both.** Her initial "Yes" addresses the burden of extensive settings, not the proposed control over adventurousness. The following improvement concerns interface complexity; it does not reverse her reservation about making the choice itself. His willingness to leave a default available does not withdraw his support for the option.

### 6. Woman

The woman explicitly gives the closer match precedence in a personal recommendation list, despite professionally supporting new artists. The man would reserve some positions for newcomers even when they are a weaker fit. This is a concrete exception to the statement's "always".

**Attractive wrong answer: Both.** He is not simply asking for unknown artists to be considered when they suit the listener equally well. He explicitly accepts a weaker match to give them exposure. Her professional advocacy, conversely, does not change her stated priority for a personal service.

## Editorial and recording notes

- This revision retains the original answer distribution and topic, but changes opinions 2 and 6 to make the relevant timing and priority distinctions precise.
- Item 3 provides a comparatively accessible point in the middle of the discussion. Item 4 requires listeners to retain agreement despite a limited final endorsement. Items 2, 5 and 6 depend on the exact scope of a claim.
- Keep the brief "Yes" in the settings exchange conversational. Do not deliver it as a conspicuous trick or insert a dramatic pause before the qualification.
- Use natural British English. Differences of view should remain collegial; avoid consistently signalling the answer through emphatic stress on contrast words.
- Review the finished recording for any emphasis or pause that changes the apparent scope of agreement. Text review alone does not establish the difficulty of the recorded task.
