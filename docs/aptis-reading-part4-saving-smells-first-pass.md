# Saving the smells of the past

First-pass classroom activity for Aptis General / Aptis ESOL General Reading Part 4.

## Activity design

Use the text below as one connected article. Paragraph 0 is a short worked example; paragraphs 1–7 are the seven student rounds. The target is identifying the main idea, rather than producing polished English.

Suggested sequence for each round:

1. Show the paragraph with the headings hidden.
2. Students submit a brief response to: **“What is this paragraph mainly saying? Explain it in your own words. If it helps, start with ‘It explains…’, ‘It describes…’ or ‘It questions…’.”** Aim for roughly 8–15 words, but accept shorter or longer responses that capture the idea. Minor language mistakes do not matter here.
3. Once the class has submitted, reveal that round’s four headings. Keep the paragraph and each student’s own response visible.
4. Students choose a heading and submit. The teacher then reveals the answer and compares a few anonymous main-idea responses. Useful follow-up: **“Does that heading cover the whole paragraph, or only something it mentions?”**
5. Move to the next paragraph when the teacher is ready.

Allow around 60–90 seconds for reading and the written response, then 20–30 seconds for choosing, followed by discussion. These are flexible classroom timings, not exam limits. Use teacher judgement for the written responses; do not require an exact match to the models below.

Keep the master heading bank hidden during these rounds. After all seven, show the complete article and the master bank for a final matching pass, with no invented classroom headings. This last pass practises the full option set, although students will already know the article; use a fresh text later to assess independent transfer.

## Student text

### Saving the smells of the past

**0 — Worked example: An overlooked part of our past**

When a historic building is restored, experts pay close attention to its walls, furniture and paintings. Yet something that once helped give the place its character may receive far less attention: its smell. The air inside a workshop, a library or a kitchen can tell a story that photographs cannot fully capture. Researchers are therefore asking whether protecting the past should include recording the scents associated with it, alongside the objects we can see and touch.

**1**

Anyone who has opened an old book will recognise its distinctive smell. It is tempting to imagine that the pages have simply collected dust over the years. In fact, as paper ages, some of its materials slowly break down and release chemicals into the air. Certain compounds can produce sweet notes also associated with vanilla, while others contribute different qualities. The exact mixture depends on the materials and their condition. What seems like a single familiar scent is therefore the result of several changes taking place inside the object itself, rather than just something added by its surroundings.

**2**

To record such a scent, scientists can place a special material near an object to collect some of the compounds it releases. Laboratory equipment then separates and identifies the substances in the sample. The resulting information provides a chemical profile, rather like a list of ingredients. Researchers can keep this record even if the original object later changes or disappears. It may also help specialists develop a similar scent in the future. Recording a smell does not necessarily mean keeping the original air in a bottle; it can mean preserving the information needed to understand what was in it.

**3**

However, a list of chemicals cannot describe the whole experience. Ask several visitors to describe the same library and one may mention wood, another vanilla and another a cupboard in a grandparent’s house. They may also disagree about whether the smell is pleasant. These responses reveal associations that a laboratory instrument cannot detect. Researchers therefore collect people’s descriptions alongside scientific measurements. A comment about a childhood home may appear less precise than a chemical name, but it helps explain the meaning the smell has for that person. Understanding the scent requires attention to both the object and the person experiencing it.

**4**

These records could give museums another way to help visitors connect with their collections. Imagine looking at the tools from an old workshop while encountering a carefully prepared scent associated with its materials. The objects might become easier to imagine in use, rather than simply resting behind glass. Smells can also encourage visitors to ask questions: why was that material used, and what would working there have been like? The purpose need not be to provide an exciting extra attraction. Used with explanation, a scent can become another route into the subject, supporting what visitors learn from objects and written information.

**5**

Recreating a smell from centuries ago raises a different problem. A surviving recipe may identify ingredients without specifying their exact quantities, while a diary might describe an aroma only as “strong” or “sweet”. Materials available today may also differ from those originally used. Even a historic room that still exists has changed through cleaning, repairs and everyday use. A convincing recreation is consequently not proof that the past smelled exactly that way. Museums can still use it, but should explain which parts are supported by evidence and which involve interpretation. Otherwise, visitors may mistake a carefully researched possibility for a complete and certain reconstruction.

**6**

There is also a temptation to preserve only the scents that people enjoy. Flowers and fresh bread may attract visitors, whereas smoke, rubbish and unwashed clothing are less inviting. Yet an exhibition that includes only pleasant aromas could offer a misleading picture of daily life. Uncomfortable smells can raise questions about working conditions, waste or the differences between rich and poor households. This does not mean making every gallery unpleasant. A small sample, offered with an explanation, might be enough. The historical importance of a smell depends on what it helps us understand, rather than whether we would choose it for our homes.

**7**

Deciding what to record should therefore involve more than a discussion among museum specialists. Residents may value the smell of a local bakery, a fishing harbour or a factory that has recently closed. Such places need not be famous to matter to the people who know them. Asking communities which scents they would miss can reveal histories that official collections overlook. Their explanations can become part of the record alongside samples and scientific information. The aim is not to save every smell everywhere, but to give people a voice in choosing which parts of their shared experience deserve to be remembered.

## Classroom heading rounds — teacher copy

Show only the four option texts to students, without the annotations. The master-bank letters below are for the teacher. Classroom option numbers restart each round; correct positions vary.

### Round 1

Suggested main-idea response: **“It explains how changes in ageing paper produce an old book’s smell.”**

1. How books absorb smells from the rooms around them
2. The processes behind a familiar smell
3. Turning a scent into a scientific record
4. The appeal of old books to modern readers

**Answer: 2 — F.** The paragraph explains where the scent comes from, including the breakdown of materials and the chemicals released. Option 1 echoes the opening assumption but misstates the main explanation. Option 3 is a real heading for paragraph 2; no recording method appears here. Option 4 sounds relevant but readers’ preferences are not the focus.

Evidence to discuss: **“as paper ages, some of its materials slowly break down and release chemicals into the air.”**

### Round 2

Suggested main-idea response: **“It describes how scientists collect and analyse a smell to record it.”**

1. Keeping historic air sealed inside containers
2. The processes behind a familiar smell
3. Using a scent to identify an object's age
4. Turning a scent into a scientific record

**Answer: 4 — B.** The paragraph follows the method from collecting compounds to keeping a chemical profile. Option 1 misreads the bottle comparison. Option 2 belongs to paragraph 1: explaining how a smell forms is different from explaining how it is documented. Option 3 invents a purpose that this paragraph does not describe.

Evidence to discuss: **“The resulting information provides a chemical profile, rather like a list of ingredients.”**

### Round 3

Suggested main-idea response: **“It explains why people’s descriptions add meaning that chemical measurements cannot provide.”**

1. What personal descriptions add to the evidence
2. Replacing laboratory measurements with visitors’ memories
3. Helping visitors connect with a collection
4. Why old libraries remind everyone of home

**Answer: 1 — H.** People's responses complement the measurements by revealing associations and judgements. Option 2 changes “alongside” into replacement. Option 3 is the real heading for paragraph 4, which discusses an educational use rather than gathering evidence. Option 4 turns one possible personal association into a claim about everyone.

Evidence to discuss: **“Researchers therefore collect people’s descriptions alongside scientific measurements.”**

### Round 4

Suggested main-idea response: **“It shows how scents can help museum visitors imagine and understand the exhibits.”**

1. Attracting larger audiences with exciting new displays
2. What personal descriptions add to the evidence
3. Helping visitors connect with a collection
4. Replacing written explanations with sensory experiences

**Answer: 3 — D.** The workshop example develops an educational benefit: imagining objects in use and asking questions about them. Option 1 promotes the attraction mentioned briefly into the paragraph's purpose, and adds an unsupported claim about audience size. Option 2 belongs to paragraph 3. Option 4 reverses the relationship: scent supports written information here.

### Round 5

Suggested main-idea response: **“It questions how accurately we can reproduce historical smells from incomplete evidence.”**

1. Why surviving recipes guarantee an accurate result
2. The limits of recreating the past
3. Turning a scent into a scientific record
4. Changes in ingredients as the main obstacle to reconstruction

**Answer: 2 — A.** Incomplete recipes, vague descriptions, different materials and altered rooms all support the same limitation. Option 1 reverses the argument. Option 3 belongs to paragraph 2; this paragraph examines the certainty of a reconstruction. Option 4 picks one genuine supporting detail and treats it as the main obstacle, which the paragraph does not establish.

Evidence to discuss: **“A convincing recreation is consequently not proof that the past smelled exactly that way.”**

### Round 6

Suggested main-idea response: **“It argues that unpleasant smells can help us understand historical life.”**

1. Giving local people a say in what survives
2. Making historic places smell more attractive
3. The dangers of smoke in historic workplaces
4. Why unpleasant smells can be worth preserving

**Answer: 4 — G.** The contrast is between selecting enjoyable smells and representing history meaningfully. Option 1 is the real heading for paragraph 7; community participation is not discussed here. Option 2 reverses the argument. Option 3 takes the smoke example and turns it into a workplace-safety topic that the paragraph does not develop.

Evidence to discuss: **“an exhibition that includes only pleasant aromas could offer a misleading picture of daily life.”**

### Round 7

Suggested main-idea response: **“It argues that communities should help decide which smells are recorded.”**

1. Giving local people a say in what survives
2. Preserving every smell before it disappears
3. Why unpleasant smells can be worth preserving
4. Choosing famous places as the priority for preservation

**Answer: 1 — C.** The paragraph is about who participates in choosing heritage smells. Option 2 contradicts the stated limit. Option 3 belongs to paragraph 6, which concerns historical value regardless of pleasantness. Option 4 reverses the point that ordinary places can also matter.

Evidence to discuss: **“Asking communities which scents they would miss can reveal histories that official collections overlook.”**

## Final answer key

**1 F · 2 B · 3 H · 4 D · 5 A · 6 G · 7 C. Unused: E.**

E is plausible because the article mentions ageing materials and protecting the past. However, no paragraph proposes removing smells to protect objects. The distinction is between preserving an object and documenting its scent.

## Notes for revision

- Intended language level: accessible B2 practice, with unfamiliar scientific ideas explained in everyday terms. This is a drafting target, not an official calibrated level.
- Each round contains the real heading, one real heading used elsewhere, and two invented classroom distractors. Invented headings are excluded from the final master bank.
- The paragraph 0 example gives context without revealing a heading needed for paragraphs 1–7. For the worked example, model a response such as: “It introduces smells as a neglected part of historical preservation.”
- Reveal the key only after the heading submissions. Treat the free responses as evidence for discussion, not as a grammar or spelling test.
- The most useful distinctions to discuss are paragraph 1 versus 2 (how a smell forms / how it is recorded), paragraph 3 versus 4 (collecting human responses / helping visitors learn), and paragraph 6 versus 7 (what makes a smell worth recording / who should help choose).
- The final full-bank match is review. Do not present its score as an independent measure of exam readiness after students have already seen the answers.

## Research and format notes — teacher only

This is an original teaching passage informed by the sources below. The workshop, bakery, harbour and factory scenarios are illustrative examples, not reports of particular experiments. No numerical research findings or invented quotations from researchers are used. The words “strong” and “sweet” in paragraph 5 illustrate imprecise descriptions; they are not quotations from a named historical document.

- [British Council: Prepare for Aptis ESOL General](https://www.britishcouncil.org/exam/english/aptis/prepare-general) describes about 750 words, eight paragraphs and seven heading matches from eight options. Here paragraph 0 is a worked example and paragraphs 1–7 are assessed. The written submissions and four-option rounds are classroom scaffolding.
- [UCL: Preserving the smells of history](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/bartlett/ideas/bartlett-review/bartlett-review-2016/preserving-smells-history) explains collecting scent profiles and analysing their compounds, and the importance of human experience alongside chemical information.
- [Bembibre and Strlič (2017): Smell of heritage](https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-016-0114-1) reports chemical and sensory analysis of historic paper and libraries. It supports the paper-ageing explanation and the distinction between chemical measurements and people's descriptions. Published under [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/); the teaching passage simplifies and reorganises the ideas.
- [UCL: Olfactory Heritage](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/bartlett/environment-energy-resources/heritage/research-institute-sustainable-heritage/heritage-science/olfactory-heritage) explains the cultural significance of smells associated with places, practices and communities.
- [Odeuropa: The Heritage Smell Library](https://odeuropa.eu/the-heritage-smell-library/) distinguishes representations based on chemical analysis from recreations based on historical recipes and other evidence, and describes documenting their cultural significance.
- [Odeuropa Encyclopedia: Historically Themed Attractions](https://encyclopedia.odeuropa.eu/items/show/22) discusses educational uses of historical scents and the need to acknowledge interpretive limits. Paragraphs 4–6 develop these questions through original classroom examples and reasoning.
